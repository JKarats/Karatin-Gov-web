Data Security Posture Management: Six Problems Hidden in Plain Sight

Six practical use cases for discovering sensitive information, excessive access, risky sharing, and unmanaged data across technology environments.

← More Data Security articles

A firewall cannot tell you whether a forgotten project workspace contains customer records open to hundreds of people. Data security posture management (DSPM) helps teams discover where sensitive information lives, understand its exposure, and prioritize improvements before misuse or accidental disclosure occurs.

DSPM complements—rather than replaces—DLP

DSPM is a practical approach to understanding the location, sensitivity, ownership, access paths, and exposure of organizational information. Discovery and posture analysis help answer what do we have, where is it, who can reach it, and why? Data loss prevention (DLP) focuses on detecting and responding to risky movement or use of that information, according to the capabilities and coverage of the deployed platform.

These approaches work together: knowing where sensitive content resides helps teams design more relevant policies; DLP activity and alerts can reveal problems worth investigating in the underlying data estate.

Use case 1 — Payroll files copied during a migration

The business problem

A team moves shared drives to a cloud repository. Historical payroll exports are duplicated into a broadly accessible staging folder.

DSPM questions: Which files contain bank information? Are duplicates still present? Who can read the staging folder? When were these permissions last reviewed?

Practical response: Classify the records, confirm HR ownership, remove redundant copies, restrict the staging area, then apply monitoring or DLP to sensitive transfers.

Measure: Number of risky copies remediated; outstanding high-exposure repositories by owner.

Use case 2 — An external guest retains access after a project closes

The business problem

A contractor's project concludes, but their guest account and historic sharing links can still reach nonpublic documents.

DSPM questions: Which sensitive folders are guest-accessible? Which links permit broad access? Is an accountable business owner assigned?

Practical response: Review and remove stale guest access, expire risky links where supported, and implement a recurring access certification process.

Measure: Stale guest permissions closed; time taken to resolve access exceptions.

Use case 3 — Sensitive customer data in an unexpected system

The business problem

Teams export customer records from an approved system into spreadsheets or analytics workspaces that have weaker safeguards.

DSPM questions: Where do the copies exist? Do they contain direct identifiers? Are they connected to an approved business process?

Practical response: Identify the business purpose, establish retention and access controls, and use classification and DLP in locations the organization can actually monitor.

Measure: Unowned sensitive repositories; recurring unauthorized copy locations.

Use case 4 — AI search makes existing oversharing easier to find

The business problem

A workplace AI assistant indexes files a user can already access. Excess permissions make confidential documents easier to surface in prompts or summaries.

DSPM questions: Which sensitive repositories have broad access? Are there stale membership groups, inherited permissions, or unlabeled files?

Practical response: Reduce oversharing before expanding assistant access, test permissions with representative users, and maintain human review of sensitive outputs.

Measure: High-risk access exposures resolved before deployment; residual exceptions requiring approval.

Use case 5 — Unclear ownership blocks remediation

The business problem

A scan identifies sensitive data, but nobody owns the folder and IT cannot decide whether to archive, restrict, or delete it.

Practical response: Assign business data stewards, document purpose and retention, and establish an escalation path for unknown ownership.

Measure: Percentage of sensitive repositories with named owners; average time to disposition.

Use case 6 — A risky location has no DLP coverage

The business problem

The organization relies on DLP, yet one line-of-business repository or file-transfer route is outside its policy scope.

Practical response: Map actual data flows, document visibility gaps, and decide whether to integrate, restrict, or compensate with other safeguards. Do not assume every product inspects every file or transfer.

Measure: Percentage of prioritized sensitive data flows with an assigned control and test result.

How to start without buying another tool

  1. Choose one high-value data set, such as payroll, proposal pricing, or customer records.
  2. Identify systems and business owners; document known copies and sharing paths.
  3. Use available platform reports, data discovery, and access review evidence.
  4. Prioritize findings by sensitivity, exposure, business impact, and control coverage.
  5. Assign remediation and confirm the result; repeat on a measured cadence.

Specialist DSPM tools can automate discovery and prioritization across platforms, but the operating discipline matters regardless of vendor.

Questions to ask before choosing a platform

Can it discover relevant repositories, recognize sensitive data with acceptable accuracy, show effective permissions, map ownership, and support remediation workflows? What systems are outside coverage? Can findings be verified, exported, and acted on by accountable owners?
