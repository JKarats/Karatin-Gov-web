Data Classification & Labeling: A Practical Foundation for Protection

A pragmatic guide to identifying sensitive data, applying understandable labels, and building a dependable foundation for data loss prevention.

Karatin LLC8 min read
Article overview ← Back to Resources Data Protection8 min read Companion article →

DLP is more effective when an organization understands what its information means, how sensitive it is, and who should use it. Classification establishes that context; consistent sensitivity labels make it easier to apply the right protections. Neither step replaces good access control, but both can reduce guesswork.

First, distinguish classification from labeling

Classification is the business decision about how sensitive information is and what harm could follow from improper disclosure. Labeling gives that decision a consistent marker in a document or email, and in supported technology the label can trigger protection behavior such as encryption, marking, or policy-based controls.

A label is not the same as a retention label: sensitivity labels primarily communicate and enforce handling/protection, while retention labels support record lifecycle requirements. The two can coexist.

Start with a simple, usable hierarchy

Illustrative tierExample informationTypical handling
PublicApproved marketing materials, published reportsExternal distribution allowed after review
InternalOrdinary project schedules and internal notesRoutine internal collaboration; avoid unrestricted public sharing
ConfidentialCustomer proposals, employee records, nonpublic budgetsNeed-to-know access; stronger sharing review, and targeted DLP
RestrictedHighly sensitive personnel, financial, legal, or contract dataStrict access, approved recipients, stronger protection, monitoring

These are illustrative classifications, not a ready-made policy. Each organization must define tiers, owners, exceptions, and legal obligations for its actual data.

Use case 1 — Government procurement documentation

A draft proposal contains nonpublic pricing and subcontractor details

Classification: Confidential until the authorized team releases it. Label: “Confidential — Procurement” or an equivalent organization-approved label.

Handling: Limit access to the bid team, review sharing with external collaborators, and apply controls to files sent to unapproved recipients. DLP can use the label as one policy condition in supported locations.

Lesson: “Government-related” does not automatically mean a document is classified national-security information. Procurement sensitivity and any actual contract-controlled information requirements must be evaluated separately.

Use case 2 — HR and employee information

A spreadsheet combines names, payroll figures, and banking details

Classification: Restricted. Label: “Restricted — HR/Payroll.”

Handling: Limit file access to authorized HR/payroll staff, consider encryption, and test DLP rules for external sharing or transfers to unmanaged locations. Where supported, use both the sensitivity label and sensitive information types to detect or protect the content.

Lesson: A generic “Internal” label would hide the real risk; clear ownership and accurate labeling support more precise controls.

Use case 3 — Commercial forecasts and product plans

A leadership deck reveals an unannounced product strategy

Classification: Confidential, even though it contains no Social Security or credit card numbers.

Handling: Classify with input from the business owner; apply a sensitivity label and test whether DLP should warn, audit, or restrict sending it outside approved groups.

Lesson: Pattern matching alone may miss business secrets. Labels can carry business meaning that content detectors cannot always infer.

How to make labeling stick

  1. Inventory high-value data first. Focus on personnel, contracts, financial, customer, and sensitive research information.
  2. Assign a data owner. Define who decides the sensitivity level and who approves exceptions.
  3. Make labels intuitive. Use a short hierarchy, realistic examples, and brief tooltip-style guidance.
  4. Decide protection behavior. Map labels to access, encryption, external sharing, and DLP controls where supported.
  5. Pilot with real users. Test Outlook, Office files, SharePoint, OneDrive, mobile access, and partner collaboration as appropriate.
  6. Automate carefully. Consider sensitive information types, classifiers, and auto-labeling where eligible; validate errors and confidence before broad enforcement.
Measures to track: label coverage in priority repositories; mislabeling and downgrade reasons; exceptions; number of sensitive files shared externally; and DLP incidents requiring investigation. Use baseline and trend data instead of claiming an arbitrary success percentage.

Remember: labels are part of a control system

Classification and labels help establish policy intent, but they do not correct excessive permissions on their own. Review identity, least-privilege access, guest sharing, and content ownership as part of the same data protection program—especially before scaling AI discovery and summarization.
